A **whisker**, or **vibrissa**, is a hair, but a hair that has been promoted to instrument.
Where ordinary fur is for warmth and show, vibrissae are for **sensing**: they are thicker,
stiffer, set more deeply in the skin, and — crucially — wired into the nervous system far more
richly than any normal hair.<sup class="cn"><a href="#">[citation needed]</a></sup>

## How a whisker feels

The whisker shaft itself is dead keratin and cannot feel anything. The sensing happens at the
root, which sits in a **follicle-sinus complex** — a follicle wrapped in a little blood-filled
cushion and surrounded by hundreds of nerve endings. When the tip of the whisker brushes
something, the shaft pivots, and the follicle reports the exact angle and force. The animal
essentially reads the world through tiny levers.

## The geometry of the cat

A cat carries about **24 main whiskers** on its muzzle, arranged in four tidy horizontal rows
on each side, plus smaller sets above the eyes, on the chin, and on the backs of the front
legs. A widely cited rule of thumb is that a cat's whisker span is roughly **as wide as its
body**, letting it judge in the dark whether a gap is wide enough to fit through — although a
well-fed cat can out-grow its own measuring sticks.

Whiskers are also expressive: pushed forward in curiosity or hunting, pinned back in fear, a
running commentary on the animal's mood.

## Beyond cats

Most mammals have them. Rats sweep their whiskers back and forth in a rhythmic motion called
**whisking** to map surfaces and textures. Seals and sea lions have spectacularly sensitive
whiskers that can track the **hydrodynamic trail** left by a fish that swam past seconds
earlier — following a meal through murky water by feeling its wake.

Humans, alas, lost theirs. We keep only the faint vestigial muscles where vibrissae once
twitched — another small souvenir of a furrier past, much like <a href="/wiki/goosebumps">goosebumps</a>.
