**Alopecia areata** is a common autoimmune condition in which the immune system, which is
supposed to attack invaders, mistakes the hair follicle for the enemy and shuts it down. The
result is sudden, **smooth, round, coin-shaped bald patches**, usually on the scalp but
possible anywhere there is hair. Around 2% of people experience it at some point.<sup class="cn"><a href="#">[citation needed]</a></sup>

## What it looks like

The patches are strikingly clean-edged — bare skin, no scaling or scarring — which is part of
how it is recognised. At the advancing edge, doctors look for **"exclamation mark" hairs**:
short, broken stubs that taper to a narrower point at the scalp, like a tiny exclamation point
standing in the bald zone.

Crucially, the follicles are **shut down, not destroyed.** That is the good news buried in the
condition: because the follicle survives, hair can return.

## Totalis and universalis

Most cases stay patchy, but the condition can be more extensive. When it takes the entire
scalp it is called **alopecia totalis**; when it clears the entire body — eyebrows, lashes, and
all — it is **alopecia universalis**. These severe forms are less likely to fully recover on
their own.

## The white-regrowth curiosity

When hair does grow back, it sometimes comes in **white at first**, regaining its color later.
The pattern is also part of the legend behind hair "turning white overnight": a sudden, diffuse
form of alopecia areata can shed the *pigmented* hairs preferentially and leave the white ones
behind, mimicking overnight whitening. The full story is in
[why hair turns gray](/wiki/why-hair-turns-gray).

## A century of waiting, then a breakthrough

For most of medical history there was no reliable treatment — just steroids, patience, and
wigs. That changed in **June 2022**, when the JAK inhibitor **baricitinib** became the first
systemic drug ever approved specifically for severe alopecia areata, with a meaningful share of
patients regrowing most of their scalp hair.[^baricitinib] A second drug, **ritlecitinib**,
followed in 2023 and was the first approved down to age 12.[^ritlecitinib]

Public awareness of the condition rose sharply in the early 2020s after it became the subject
of a very high-profile awards-show moment — proof that even a quiet autoimmune condition can
end up in the headlines.
